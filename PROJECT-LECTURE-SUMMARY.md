# Project Lecture Summary

Research strategy for the ENGGEN 403 Team Project. Read this before starting any research.
It is the source the `impact-research-lanes` skill encodes.

---

## Start from what the research has to feed

Research that does not land in one of these is wasted time you do not have:

* The two problem statements, specifically the "why it exists" and the "negative consequence" elements
* Stakeholder positions and their requirements
* Justification for the critical success factors
* DFV support, especially feasibility precedent and cost order of magnitude
* The one monetised impact per package
* Context in the strategic case, where the values debate belongs

Before anyone opens a database, decide which of those a given search is serving. "Learn about speed limits" is not a research task. "Find the effect size for urban limit reductions on serious injuries, and whether it transfers to NZ" is.

## Size it correctly

You were told twice this is not a research paper, and given eight to ten hours per person. Thirty people each doing four hours of reading produces 120 hours of incompatible notes and no report.

Run a research cell of four to six people. They start Monday 8am, work through Monday, and deliver an evidence brief to the whole team by Tuesday morning so the framing and stakeholder sub-teams can use it. Everyone else reads the brief and the eleven references, and does not go hunting independently. If someone else needs a fact, they request it from the cell rather than sourcing it themselves. That single rule is what keeps the reference list coherent.

Everything the cell produces goes through the evidence ledger format, so it merges instead of colliding.

## The eleven references are the spine

Read those first. They are pre-vetted, the markers know them, and citing them signals you engaged with what you were given. Gossling et al 2023 on the German 130 km/h case, Yannis and Michelaraki 2024 and 2025 on city-wide 30 km/h in Europe, Quddus et al 2025 on UK 20 mph, Kingham 2022 and 2024, Welch 2025, the UK DfT release, the Auckland Transport safe speeds evidence page, and the NZTA reversals page.

One thing to notice about that list, because it changes what you should do next. It is not balanced. The peer-reviewed studies in it point one way. The strongest pro-increase source is a UK government press release, which is the weakest source type on the list. Kingham and Welch are opinion and commentary pieces from people with a stated position, which does not make them wrong but does make them advocacy rather than evidence. If your report's entire evidence base is this list, it will read to a Minister on the productivity side as having only consulted one side.

So the highest-value research task of the week is deliberately sourcing the strongest available case for higher limits. Look for the actual modelling behind the productivity argument, the Ministry's own regulatory impact analysis for the 2024 rule change, and the NZTA consultation material. Steelman it. That is the "critically assess evidence" objective the course put first, and it is also what makes the report credible to the reader who matters.

## The claims worth chasing

Roughly in order of value to your report:

**Effect size, and whether it transfers.** How much does an urban limit reduction change deaths and serious injuries. The European and UK studies give you numbers, but transferability to a different fleet, road environment and enforcement regime is the weak link in the chain and the thing most likely to be challenged. Record it as an assumption if you cannot evidence it.

**Compliance without physical change.** Whether observed speeds actually fall when a sign changes but the road does not. This determines whether signage-only options survive your DFV screen, so it directly shapes the economic case. Auckland Transport's evidence base is the obvious place to start.

**The time savings question.** Both the magnitude and whether saved time converts to economic value. Gossling et al is your best peer-reviewed anchor here.

**The BCR constraint itself.** The 2024 rule requires a benefit cost ratio counting only safety and time savings, road by road. That constraint is arguably a better problem statement than the limits, because it structurally excludes the benefits your client cares about. Get the actual rule wording from the NZTA page rather than relying on the video.

**Consultation cost.** Road-by-road versus bulk. If you can find or reasonably estimate a per-road consultation cost, it feeds both a problem statement and a DFV viability estimate.

**The monetisation value.** One value, sourced. NZTA's social cost of death and serious injury if you go the safety route. Get it from the source document, not from a summary and not from an AI.

## Where to look beyond the brief

Library databases for the peer-reviewed layer, since Google Scholar will paywall you and you have a UoA login. Agency primary sources for anything about what the rule actually says: NZTA, the Ministry, council speed management plans, Treasury for CBAx. Regulatory impact statements are underused by students and are exactly the kind of document that shows both sides of an argument with numbers attached.

Use AI as a search engine and orientation tool, which is what you were explicitly told to do, then verify anything you intend to cite against the actual source. Never cite something an AI told you without opening it. A fabricated or misattributed reference is trivially checkable by a marker and it costs you more credibility than the fact was worth.

## Handling the contested stuff

Most of the interesting claims will be contested. That is the assignment, not a failure of your research. Classify honestly, present both sides where they exist, and say which way the weight of evidence falls and why. A report that finds everything conveniently supports the client is less persuasive than one that says "the safety evidence is strong, the transferability is uncertain, and here is what we would need to resolve it".

Where evidence runs out, an assumption is the correct output. The deliverable document names transferability of overseas experience to New Zealand as an example assumption, so you are expected to hit this.

## Timebox

**Monday:** cell reads the eleven, ledgers them, and actively sources the productivity-side case. Evidence brief to the team Monday evening or Tuesday 8am.

**Tuesday:** targeted follow-ups only, driven by what the framing and stakeholder work has revealed it needs.

**Wednesday onward:** research is closed except for two things, feasibility precedent for specific options and the monetisation value. If someone is still reading widely on Thursday, something has gone wrong upstream.

## Traps

Researching the topic instead of the claims. Reading the example reports for content rather than scope. Letting everyone cite independently, which produces four formats and three duplicate entries for the same study. Citing the video rather than the underlying sources, since Kingham's video is orientation, not a citable evidence base. And treating the brief's reference list as complete, which is the specific trap this year's list sets.

---

Once the two draft problem statements exist, work backwards from them to the specific claim list they need evidenced. That is a tighter research brief than anything that can be written before the statements exist.
