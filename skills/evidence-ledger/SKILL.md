# Evidence Ledger

On contested policy topics most of what circulates is assertion presented with the confidence of evidence. Both sides do it. A report that repeats assertions loses credibility with exactly the reader it needs to persuade, and a report that classifies them honestly gains credibility even when it recommends against that reader's position.

This skill produces one ledger entry per claim, in a fixed format, so that work split across many people merges into a single consistent evidence base rather than thirty incompatible notes.

## What counts as what

**Evidenced.** A causal or factual claim supported by primary research, official statistics, or a systematic review, where the study design can actually support the claim being made. Note that a well-designed study supporting a narrower claim does not make the broader claim evidenced. Record the narrower claim.

**Contested.** Credible evidence exists on more than one side, or the evidence is sound but its applicability to this context is disputed. This is a real and respectable category. Most interesting policy claims live here, and saying so is not fence-sitting.

**Unsupported assertion.** A claim advanced without evidence, or supported only by sources that are restating the same original assertion. Includes claims that are true but irrelevant to the conclusion drawn from them.

**Value position.** Not a factual claim at all. "Individuals should judge for themselves what speed is safe" is a position about responsibility, not a fact that can be checked. Classify it as such rather than trying to evidence it. Value positions belong in stakeholder analysis and the context section.

Watch for the mixed case, which is the most common: a factual core wrapped in a value conclusion. Split them and classify each part.

## Source appraisal

For every source record:

- **Type.** Peer-reviewed study, systematic review, government agency report, agency modelling, advocacy publication, news, opinion, blog.
- **Who produced it and what position they hold.** Not to dismiss it. Advocacy and industry sources are often the only holders of certain data. Record the interest so the reader can weigh it.
- **Jurisdiction and date.** Transferability across countries is frequently the weakest link in a policy evidence chain, and evidence from a different road environment, fleet age or enforcement regime may not transfer.
- **What it actually measures.** Distinguish modelled from observed. Modelled results inherit their assumptions, so record the key assumption if it is stated.
- **Chain check.** Follow the citation back at least one step. A widely repeated figure often traces to a single old source or to a body citing itself.

## Ledger entry format

One block per claim, always this shape.

```
CLAIM: [the claim in one sentence, as the team would state it in the report]
CLASSIFICATION: evidenced / contested / unsupported assertion / value position
SOURCE: [author, org, year, title]
TYPE: [source type]
POSITION OF PRODUCER: [interest held, or "no evident position"]
JURISDICTION AND DATE: [where, when, whether it transfers to this context]
MEASURES: [observed or modelled, of what, over what period]
STRENGTH: strong / moderate / weak, with the reason
COUNTER-EVIDENCE: [what argues the other way, or "none found — searched X"]
USE IN REPORT: [which section, and whether it can carry a recommendation or only context]
APA 7: [full reference entry]
```

If a field cannot be filled, write "unknown" rather than omitting the line. A visible gap is information; a missing line looks like an oversight.

## Rules that keep the ledger honest

Record counter-evidence even when it weakens the client's preferred position. A report that never surfaces anything inconvenient reads as advocacy and will be discounted by an audience that already holds the other view.

Never upgrade a classification to make a section easier to write. If the claim you need is contested, write the section as contested. The marks and the credibility are both in the honest handling.

Do not generate citations from memory. If you have not seen the source, say so and mark the entry as needing verification. Fabricated or half-remembered references are the fastest way to lose a marker's trust, and they are easy to check.

When several ledger entries cover the same claim, merge them into one entry with multiple sources rather than duplicating, and note where the sources disagree.

## Batch mode

When given a set of sources or a research assignment covering several claims, produce the entries in one table sorted by claim, then a short synthesis noting: which claims are solid enough to carry a recommendation, which are contested and need both sides presented, and which the team should stop using.
