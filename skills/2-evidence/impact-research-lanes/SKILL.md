# Impact Research Lanes

Part (a) of the brief is "economic versus social versus environmental impacts of speed limit increases". The word doing the work is *versus*. What the three lanes owe the report is a comparison showing where they pull against each other and what that costs, not three literature reviews stapled together.

This skill does two jobs. It converts research intentions into specific claims with a named destination, and it keeps parallel work in one format so it merges instead of colliding.

Research lanes produce evidence. They do not choose options. That happens later, in screening, using this evidence.

## Every search serves a destination

Research that does not land in one of these is wasted time the team does not have:

1. The two problem statements, specifically the "why it exists" and the "negative consequence" elements
2. Stakeholder positions and their requirements
3. Justification for the critical success factors
4. DFV support, especially feasibility precedent and cost order of magnitude
5. The one monetised impact per package
6. Context in the strategic case, where the values debate belongs

Before anyone opens a database, the destination is decided. "Learn about speed limits" is not a research task. "Find the effect size for urban limit reductions on serious injuries, and whether it transfers to New Zealand" is.

When a user brings a broad intention, do not start searching. Convert it first: name the destination, state the claim as a sentence that could be true or false, and say what unit the answer has to arrive in. If it cannot be converted, it is not research, and saying so saves more time than any search will.

## Size and sequence

Eight to ten hours per student, and the brief says twice this is not a research paper. Thirty people each reading for four hours produces a hundred and twenty hours of incompatible notes and no report.

Run a research cell of four to six people. The cell starts Monday morning and delivers an evidence brief to the whole team by Tuesday morning, so the framing and stakeholder sub-teams can build on it. Everyone else reads the brief and the eleven references and does not go hunting independently. When someone outside the cell needs a fact, they request it from the cell rather than sourcing it themselves. That one rule is what keeps the reference list coherent.

Monday: the cell reads the eleven, ledgers them, and actively sources the productivity-side case. Tuesday: targeted follow-ups only, driven by what the framing and stakeholder work has shown it needs. Wednesday onward research is closed except for feasibility precedent on specific options and the monetisation value. If someone is still reading widely on Thursday, something has gone wrong upstream and the fix is not more reading.

Everything the cell produces goes through the evidence ledger format.

## The eleven references, and what is wrong with them

Read the brief's reference list first. Those sources are pre-vetted, the markers know them, and citing them signals the team engaged with what it was given. They cover the German 130 km/h costing, the European city-wide 30 km/h reviews, the UK 20 mph evaluation, New Zealand commentary, the UK productivity release, Auckland Transport's safe speeds evidence base and the NZTA reversals page.

The list is not balanced, and this changes what to do next. The peer-reviewed studies in it point one way. The strongest pro-increase source on it is a government press release, which is the weakest source type present. The New Zealand commentary pieces are written by people with a stated position, which does not make them wrong but does make them advocacy rather than evidence. A report whose entire evidence base is this list will read, to a Minister on the productivity side, as having consulted only one side.

**So the highest-value research task of the week is deliberately sourcing the strongest available case for higher limits.** Look for the modelling behind the productivity argument, the Ministry's regulatory impact analysis for the 2024 rule change, and the NZTA consultation material. Steelman it rather than collecting it to knock down. This is the "critically assess evidence" objective the course listed first, and it is what makes the report credible to the reader who matters.

Treat the eleven as a spine, never as complete. That is the specific trap this year's list sets.

## The claims worth chasing

Roughly in order of value to the report.

**Effect size, and whether it transfers.** How much an urban limit reduction changes deaths and serious injuries. The European and UK studies supply numbers; transferability to a different fleet, road environment and enforcement regime is the weak link and the thing most likely to be challenged. Where it cannot be evidenced, record it as an assumption — the deliverables document names exactly this as an example assumption, so the team is expected to hit it.

**Compliance without physical change.** Whether observed speeds actually fall when a sign changes but the road does not. This decides whether signage-only options survive the DFV screen, so it shapes the economic case directly. Auckland Transport's evidence base is the obvious start.

**The time savings question.** Both the magnitude and whether saved time converts into economic value. The German 130 km/h study is the best peer-reviewed anchor.

**The BCR constraint itself.** The 2024 rule requires a benefit cost ratio counting only safety and time savings, road by road. That constraint is arguably a better problem statement than the limits themselves, because it structurally excludes the benefits the client cares about. Get the actual rule wording from the NZTA page rather than from the briefing video.

**Consultation cost, road-by-road versus bulk.** A per-road consultation cost, found or reasonably estimated, feeds both a problem statement and a DFV viability estimate.

**The monetisation value.** One value, sourced. NZTA's social cost of death and serious injury if the team goes the safety route. From the source document — not a summary, not an AI.

## The direction trap

The team is researching speed limit **increases**. Almost the entire evidence base evaluates **decreases**: the 2020 reductions, city-wide 30 km/h, 20 mph schemes. The 2024 reversals are recent and thinly evaluated.

An increase is not a decrease with the sign flipped. Reinstating a higher limit on a road recently lowered is a different intervention from never having lowered it, because infrastructure, signage, enforcement posture and driver expectation from the first change persist. Compliance behaviour is asymmetric and speed-injury relationships are non-linear, so a proportional effect measured going down does not transfer going up.

Record every finding with the direction actually studied. Where an inference to increases is made, mark it as an inference rather than a result.

## Ask before producing anything

Do not write the note or the search plan until the essentials are settled. Ask up to four questions, in one message, covering only what cannot be inferred from what the user has given you. Then wait.

Draw from what is actually missing:

- **Which destination does this serve?** One of the six above. Ask this first; it is the question that most often turns a day of reading into an hour.
- **Cell or requester?** Whether the user is in the research cell or needs a fact from it. A requester gets routed to the cell, not given a search strategy.
- **Which lane, and which second question.** Every team does part (a) plus one of (b) Europe and UK lessons, (c) technological traffic management beyond speed limits, or (d) urban, regional and rural interplay. The second question reshapes the lane: (b) makes transferability central, (c) pulls the economic lane towards implementation and capital cost, (d) means every finding needs its road environment recorded or it is unusable.
- **Network scope and baseline.** Which road environments are in scope, and whether the do-nothing baseline is the post-2024 reversal state or the pre-2020 state. These give different answers and the team must pick one.
- **Monetisation candidate or not.** Only one impact per package is monetised. A finding headed for that slot has to arrive in a unit CBAx accepts; everything else only needs qualifying.

Two rules on asking. Ask only what changes what you produce, because a question whose answer would not alter the output is not worth the user's time on a five-day project. And if the user says to proceed anyway, or does not know, do not stall: state the assumption in one line at the top of the output, mark it as needing confirmation, and carry on.

## Working backwards from problem statements

Once the two draft problem statements exist, they generate a tighter research brief than anything written before them. When given the statements, work backwards: for each, take the "why it exists" element and the "negative consequence" element, and derive the specific claims each one needs evidenced to survive a reader who disagrees. Produce that as a ranked claim list with destinations attached, and mark which claims the existing eleven can already carry and which need new sourcing.

Offer this whenever a user is researching without problem statements in hand and the statements are close. Waiting a few hours for them is usually cheaper than researching around them.

## Parameters all three lanes share

Settle once, for the whole team, and repeat at the top of every note. If a lane needs different ones, that is a change for all three lanes, not a local decision.

Road environments in scope. The do-nothing baseline and its date. Appraisal period and discount rate. The denominator that rates are expressed against, whether population, vehicle-kilometres or road-kilometres. Units and price year for any dollar figure.

A finding without its denominator is the most common unusable output here. "Crashes fell by 12%" cannot be combined with anything until you know per what, over what period, against what baseline.

## What sits in each lane

The brief's description of the Ministry's national model lists the impacts of interest: travel duration, distance, emissions, network volumes, fairness, safety and satisfaction, by and on whom. Use that as the spine rather than inventing categories.

**Economic.** Travel time and journey reliability, the productivity argument driving the case for increases, vehicle operating costs, freight effects, enforcement and signage cost, consultation cost, infrastructure capital and maintenance, and the fiscal position of whoever pays. The productivity claim is the headline argument for increases and is directly contested within the brief's own list, so it belongs in the ledger as contested, not as background. And a transfer is not a net impact: a fine moves money between parties and is recorded as a distributional fact.

**Social.** Safety outcomes as they fall on people, by severity and road user type. Fairness, meaning who bears the risk against who receives the time saving, rarely the same group. Severance and access, mode shift, who can use a street safely, public acceptance and compliance behaviour. The characteristic failure is drifting into advocacy, because the material is emotive and much of it comes from campaigning organisations on both sides. The brief asks the team to separate evidence from the values-driven debate, and this is the lane where that discipline is tested.

**Environmental.** Greenhouse gas emissions, local air quality as a separate matter from carbon, noise, fuel and energy use. Land take and construction footprint only where an option involves physical works. The characteristic failure is assuming a straight line: fuel burn and emissions per kilometre against speed is a curve with a minimum, so whether an increase raises or lowers emissions depends on where on that curve the road sits. Find the curve and state which part applies.

## Where the lanes overlap

**One home for each impact.** A death has a social dimension and a monetised cost. Decide which lane owns it for totalling, record it once there, cross-reference from the other. Where an impact appears in the CBAx database, that structure decides ownership, because a total assembled on a different structure cannot be checked against anything.

**Route, do not drop.** When a lane turns up something belonging to another, hand it over with the source attached and note the handoff. Findings at the edge of a lane are usually the valuable ones, and they are what a parallel split loses.

## Units the monetisation will accept

CBAx monetises; it does not quantify. It supplies a dollar value per unit and expects the analyst to supply the volume, per cohort member per year against a counterfactual. A finding intended for monetisation must arrive as a countable quantity, not a percentage change with no base.

The CBAx impact database carries values directly relevant here, including the social cost of fatal, serious and minor road crashes from Te Manatū Waka, health system costs of fatal and serious crashes, Value of a Statistical Life, health loss from NOx, CO, PM2.5 and volatile organic compounds, property value change per additional decibel of noise, shadow emissions values for CO₂ across low, central and high price paths, and Auckland congestion costs. Check the Database tab for the current list and price year before assuming a value exists.

Two consequences before searching. Crash findings are recorded as counts by severity, because that is the shape the crash values take; emissions findings need tonnes of CO₂-equivalent and air quality findings tonnes by pollutant, because carbon and air quality are valued separately. And there is no travel time value in the CBAx database, so the economic lane's headline benefit needs a value sourced and justified from elsewhere or has to be handled as a qualified impact. Settle that early rather than discovering it on Thursday.

CBAx allocates by Treasury wellbeing domain while the report qualifies impacts against the Four Capitals. Related, not the same list. Record both.

## Where to look beyond the eleven

The university library databases for the peer-reviewed layer, since Google Scholar paywalls what a UoA login opens. Agency primary sources for anything about what the rule actually says: NZTA, the Ministry, council speed management plans, Treasury for CBAx. Regulatory impact statements are underused by students and are exactly the document type that shows both sides of an argument with numbers attached.

Use AI as a search engine and orientation tool, which is what the course explicitly permits, then verify anything you intend to cite against the actual source. Never cite something an AI produced without opening the source. A fabricated or misattributed reference is trivially checkable by a marker and costs more credibility than the fact was worth.

Two currency checks. Transport policy functions that sat with the Ministry of Transport have been absorbed into a larger Ministry of Cities, Environment, Regions and Transport, so sources will name bodies and frameworks that have since moved. And the 2024 reversals postdate much of the evidence base.

## Handling contested claims

Most claims worth chasing will be contested. That is the assignment, not a research failure. Classify honestly, present both sides where they exist, and say which way the weight of evidence falls and why. A report finding that everything conveniently supports the client is less persuasive than one saying the safety evidence is strong, the transferability uncertain, and here is what would resolve it.

Where evidence runs out, an assumption is the correct output, not a gap to paper over.

## Output format

```
DESTINATION: problem statement / stakeholder / CSF / DFV / monetisation / context
LANE: economic / social / environmental
SECOND QUESTION: b / c / d
PARAMETERS: [road environments, baseline and date, appraisal period, denominator, units and price year]
ASSUMPTIONS PROCEEDING UNDER: [any unanswered clarifying question, or "none"]

FINDINGS
[one block per finding]
  CLAIM: [stated so it could be true or false, with denominator and period]
  DIRECTION STUDIED: increase / decrease / reversal — inference to increases marked as inference
  MECHANISM: [why this happens, not merely that it correlates]
  SOURCE: [author, org, year] — opened: yes / no
  ROAD ENVIRONMENT: [urban, regional, rural, motorway — and whether it transfers to ours]
  CONFIDENCE: strong / moderate / weak, with the reason
  COUNTER: [what argues the other way, or "none found — searched X"]
  QUANTIFIED AS: [countable unit and volume, or "qualitative only"]
  CBAX IMPACT: [matching database impact, or "none — needs external value" or "not for monetisation"]
  CAPITAL AND WELLBEING DOMAIN: [Four Capitals label, and CBAx wellbeing domain if monetised]

BECOMES AN ASSUMPTION: [claims the evidence could not settle, phrased ready for section 2.4]
HANDOFFS: [findings belonging to another lane, and to whom]
GAPS: [what was searched for and not found, and what the team should do about it]
```

Every finding reaching the report then goes through the evidence ledger for classification, appraisal and its APA 7 entry. This note is the research record; the ledger is the citable record.

## Building the comparison

When the lanes come together the output is the *versus*. Check in this order and stop at the first failure, because everything downstream of a parameter mismatch is unreliable.

Parameters identical across lanes. No impact totalled twice. Transfers marked as transfers. Every finding carrying a denominator and a period, and the direction actually studied, with reduction-derived inferences still visibly marked.

Then find the trade-offs, because they are what the report is marked on. Name every place the lanes point in opposite directions: where the time saving the economic lane values is paid for in the safety outcome the social lane counts, where the emissions effect runs with or against both. Surface these rather than averaging them, and where a trade-off falls on different groups, say which groups — fairness is one of the impacts the brief names, and it is what separates a comparison from a tally.

A merged output with no conflicts means the lanes stopped too early, or a disagreement was resolved quietly during the merge. Both are worse than an honest trade-off stated plainly.

## Traps

Researching the topic instead of the claims. Reading the example reports for content rather than scope. Letting everyone cite independently, which produces four formats and three duplicate entries for one study. Citing the briefing video rather than the underlying sources, since the video is orientation and not a citable evidence base. And treating the brief's reference list as complete.
