# Report House Style

When thirty people write one report, the reader notices the seams before they notice the analysis. Inconsistent terminology, register that swings between memo and essay, and three different citation formats all signal a document nobody owned. This skill applies one pass that removes the seams without rewriting the substance.

## Scope of the pass

Edit for voice, terminology, register and mechanics. Do not change what a section claims, do not add or remove evidence, and do not soften a recommendation. If a passage is substantively wrong or unsupported, flag it separately rather than fixing it silently. The author needs to know.

## Audience and register

Establish, before editing, who the section is written for. Advisory reports usually have two audiences at once: the client who commissioned it, and the decision maker the client will forward it to. Write to the client, but assume the decision maker reads it, which means:

- No language that assumes the reader already agrees with the client's position.
- Recommendations stated plainly, with the reasoning visible, so a reader who disagrees can see exactly where they part company.
- No internal team language, no course jargon, no references to the assignment or the marking.

The register is professional adviser: direct, specific, unhedged where the evidence is solid and explicitly qualified where it is not. Not academic essay, not consultancy sales deck.

## What to cut

**Fluff.** Abstract noun stacks, throat-clearing openers ("It is important to note that"), and sentences that restate the heading. Cut them entirely rather than trimming.

**Empty intensifiers.** "Significant", "substantial", "considerable" and similar carry no information unless a number follows. Either attach the number or delete the word.

**False hedging.** "It could be argued that", "some may suggest", "arguably". If the point is contested, name who contests it and on what basis. If it is not, state it.

**Unowned claims.** Passive constructions that hide who did or concluded something. Name the agent.

**Solution language in problem sections.** Strategic case sections that describe problems in terms of missing solutions have leaked scope. Flag these for the author.

## What to standardise

Keep a running terminology list for the document and apply it uniformly. On first use give the full name with any abbreviation in brackets, then use the short form consistently. Organisations that have been renamed or restructured need a decision recorded once: which name the report uses, and whether historical references use the name current at the time. Job titles, statute names and programme names get the same treatment.

Numbers, dates, units and currency follow one convention throughout. Tables and figures are numbered in one sequence each, captioned above for tables and below for figures, and every one of them is referred to by number in the body text. A figure nobody points at is decoration.

## Team conventions

These are the defaults. Adjust them once for the team, then apply without further discussion.

- New Zealand English spelling: "organisation", "prioritise", "metre", "programme" for a scheme.
- APA 7 for in-text citations and the reference list.
- Active voice as default.
- No em dashes. Use a comma, a colon, or a full stop.
- Serial comma only where needed to prevent ambiguity.
- One space after a full stop.
- Headings in sentence case.
- Acronyms defined at first use in the body, and again in the executive summary if the summary is designed to stand alone.

## Document format specification

Apply these when checking a draft for submission readiness.

- A4 only, portrait, with landscape acceptable for charts. No A3 or other special pages.
- Margins 2.00 cm.
- Body font easy to read, 11 pt or larger, line spacing between 1.15 and 2.0.
- Headings on every section of work, titles on every table and figure, page numbers throughout.
- Every table and figure referred to at least once in the body text.
- No large unbroken blocks of text.
- APA 7 in-text citations and a reference list at the end.

Where a page limit applies, note what it excludes. References, coversheet and table of contents typically sit outside it while charts and figures sit inside, and content pushed past the limit is usually not assessed at all, so overflow is lost work rather than a minor penalty. Flag page-budget risk early rather than at the proofread.

## Executive summary handling

A standalone executive summary follows different rules from the body and should be checked separately. It must make sense to someone who reads nothing else: the problem, who it affects, the recommendation, and the reason for it, with no forward references to later sections and no undefined terms. If more than about a third of it is background rather than recommendation, tell the author to rebalance.

## Output format

Return the edited text first, then a change log:

```
## Edited text
[full edited section]

## Changes made
[grouped by type: terminology, register, mechanics, cuts. Brief, not line by line.]

## Flagged for the author
[substantive issues not fixed: unsupported claims, scope leaks, missing citations, contradictions with other sections.]

## Terminology added to the document list
[any new term, with the chosen form.]
```

Keep the change log short. The author needs to know what class of thing changed, not to review every comma.

## Consistency across sections

When editing a later section, apply the terminology and conventions already established rather than starting fresh. If the user has not supplied earlier sections, ask for the terminology list, or ask which convention to treat as canonical where two sections conflict. Silently picking one and moving on creates the exact problem this pass exists to solve.
