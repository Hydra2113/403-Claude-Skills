# Standalone Executive Summary

The executive summary is the first and often only page read by the decision maker. On the ENGGEN 403 team project it carries 30% of the mark for a single page, which makes it the highest-value page in the document by a wide margin. Teams routinely write it last, in a rush, from whatever the body happens to say. That is the wrong order of effort.

## Hard constraints

300 to 500 words is typical, and it may exceed 500 words but must not exceed a single page. Items appear in the same order they occur in the body. It must close with the proposed shortlist, so a reader who stops here still sees that the team has thought about what happens next.

## What it must contain

Purpose, methods, recommendations and costs, in brief. In practice that means:

1. What the client asked for and what this report does.
2. The problem, stated specifically enough to be recognisable, covering both problem statements.
3. How the analysis was done, in a sentence or two. Not a methodology section, just enough that the reader knows the recommendation came from somewhere.
4. The critical success factors, or at least what success was judged against.
5. The recommended shortlist: the packages, what they contain, what they cost at order of magnitude, and the monetised impact.
6. What the client should do next.

Most of the weight goes on the recommendations and why. That is the point being driven home.

## The stand-alone test

Apply this literally. Cover the rest of the report and read only the summary. Then ask whether a reader could make a decision without reading the body in detail.

Specific failures that break it:

- **Forward references.** "As discussed in Section 3", "the options outlined below". The reader does not have below.
- **Undefined terms and acronyms.** Every acronym is redefined here even if defined in the body, because this page is read alone.
- **Recommendations without their reason.** Naming the packages is not recommending them. The reader needs enough of the why to disagree in an informed way.
- **Unnamed quantities.** "Significant safety benefits" tells a decision maker nothing. If the body monetised an impact, the number belongs here.
- **Content with no home in the body.** Anything in the summary must be traceable to a body section, or it is a new claim in the wrong place.

## The balance test

Count the sentences. If more than roughly a third is background and problem description, it is unbalanced. Background exists to make the recommendation intelligible, not to demonstrate that research happened. Cut background before cutting recommendation.

## Audience

Written for the client, in the knowledge that the client forwards it to a decision maker who may hold the opposite view on the underlying question. That means no language assuming agreement, no advocacy framing, and no rhetorical questions. State the recommendation and the evidence for it, and let a reader who disagrees see exactly where they part company. A summary that reads as a lobbying document is discounted by the one reader it most needs.

Never address the marker, never mention the course, the assignment or the team's process.

## Output format

When drafting:

```
## Draft executive summary
[the summary itself, word count noted]

## Traceability
| Claim in summary | Body section it comes from |

## Stand-alone check
[forward references, undefined terms, unnamed quantities, unsupported claims]

## Balance
[proportion background vs recommendation]
```

When auditing an existing draft, lead with the stand-alone check and the balance, then the traceability gaps, then wording. Wording is the least of it.

## Order of work

Draft the summary once the shortlist exists, not at the end. It surfaces gaps in the body while there is still time to fix them: if the recommendation cannot be stated crisply in three sentences, the economic case has not resolved, and that is worth knowing on Wednesday rather than Friday afternoon.
